Reference: what the standard says about synced passkeys

The NIST analysis behind our claim boundary, quoted so a reviewer can check it.

Status: ANALYSIS COMPLETE — awaiting independent review. This document is the reviewer’s input.

S6 asked a precise question: the standards treat device-bound and synced passkeys differently, there is a specific document about the synced kind, and we need to know what it says about our configuration before claiming anything publicly.

This is that analysis. Every claim is traced to a quotation from the primary source, so a reviewer can check it without trusting the author. It has not been independently reviewed, and until it has, the claims below are ours and not an assessment. That is the whole point of the slice.

1. The primary source

NIST SP 800-63B-4, Digital Identity Guidelines: Authentication and Lifecycle Management, published 2025-08-26. Two parts matter:

  • §2 (AAL) — what each assurance level requires of an authenticator.
  • Appendix B, “Syncable Authenticators” — marked normative, and the document S6 was about.

Retrieved from https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html and https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b/syncable/.

A note on scope. SP 800-63B is written for US federal agencies, but it is the reference auditors reach for outside government too. Nothing here is a certification, and this document does not claim compliance.

2. The decisive sentence

From Appendix B, “Cloning Authentication Keys”:

“Syncing authentication keys inherently means that the key can be exported. Authentication at AAL2 may be supported subject to the above requirements. However, syncing violates the non-exportability requirements of AAL3. Similar protocols using keys not stored in an exportable manner that meet the other requirements of AAL3 may be used.”

Read with §2.3.3 (AAL3 requirements):

“The cryptographic authenticator used at AAL3 SHALL have a non-exportable private key and SHALL provide phishing resistance…”

and §3.1.7.1 (multi-factor cryptographic authenticators):

“Exportable authentication keys (usable at AAL2 or below) SHOULD be stored in appropriate storage… Non-exportable authentication keys (usable at AAL3 or below) SHALL be stored in an isolated execution environment that is protected by hardware…”

The conclusion is not ambiguous: a synced passkey can never be an AAL3 authenticator. Not because it is weak, but because sync is export by definition.

This is the single most important fact for our claims, and it is the reason the two-realm split is worth having.

3. What our configuration actually is

Read from lab/keycloak/scripts/configure-realms.py, which asserts each setting persisted:

Setting Customer realm (attest-users) Privileged realm (attest-privileged)
Attestation conveyance none direct
Authenticator attachment not specified cross-platform
User verification preferred required
Discoverable credential required required
AAGUID allowlist [] — any authenticator YubiKey 5 series only
Synced passkey possible? Yes, by design No

The privileged realm asks for a cross-platform authenticator matching a hardware AAGUID, with attestation and user verification required. That is a device-bound key from a known manufacturer. The customer realm asks for almost nothing, deliberately, so that ordinary people can enrol the passkey their phone or laptop already provides.

So the split is aligned with the standard’s own boundary, and not by accident: the privileged realm is where we can make an AAL3-shaped claim, and the customer realm is where we must not.

4. What we may claim, and what we may not

We may say

  • “Privileged access requires a hardware-bound authenticator, enforced by an AAGUID allowlist and an attachment restriction, and the enrolment is refused if either is not satisfied.” — This is tested (S3), and it is what the standard asks for at AAL3.
  • “Synced passkeys are permitted only for customer accounts, where the assurance target is AAL2. The standard explicitly permits synced authenticators up to AAL2.”
  • “Sign-in is phishing-resistant in both realms.” Attested by S9, and structural: WebAuthn credentials are scoped to the relying-party origin.

We must not say

  • “AAL3 compliant”, “NIST AAL3 certified”, or anything similar. We have not performed a FIPS 140 validation of the authenticators we accept, we have not had the configuration assessed, and AAL3 requires more than WebAuthn settings. This remains true even though our WebAuthn configuration is consistent with AAL3’s authenticator requirements.
  • “AAL2 compliant” as a blanket statement about the system, for the reason in the next section.
  • Anything at all about the device the customer uses, because we do not inspect it.

5. Two findings that a reviewer should attack first

These are the parts most likely to be wrong, and they are the reason this document exists.

Finding 1 — preferred user verification means we may be accepting single factor without knowing

SP 800-63B Appendix B, “Implementation Requirements”, on the WebAuthn User Verified (UV) flag:

“Verifiers SHALL indicate that UV is preferred and SHALL inspect responses to confirm the value of the UV flag. This indicates whether the authenticator can be treated as a multi-factor cryptographic authenticator. If the user is not verified, agencies SHALL treat the authenticator as a single-factor cryptographic authenticator.“

Our customer realm sets user verification to preferred, and nothing in apps/api/src/ inspects UV, amr, or any equivalent claim. Verified by search: there are no references.

What that means in the worst case: a customer whose authenticator does not perform user verification may be authenticating with one factor, and we have no way to tell. NIST says such an assertion SHALL be treated as single-factor. AAL2 requires two distinct factors. We may therefore be presenting a single-factor sign-in as AAL2-equivalent.

This does not affect the privileged realm, where UV is required and the authenticator is hardware.

Two pieces of our own evidence make this stronger, not weaker.

First, the S3 spike explicitly recorded that “the passwordless policy already defaults to required“ — and the customer realm overrides that default down to preferred. So this is not an unexamined Keycloak default; it is a choice we made, and the spike noted the default being departed from.

Second, every virtual authenticator in this project’s harnesses is created with isUserVerified: true (console-e2e.mjs, client-isolation.mjs, userhandle-probe.mjs). The non-verified path has therefore never been exercised by any experiment we have run. We do not know whether a UV-less assertion completes sign-in in this configuration — we know only that the configuration permits it and that we would not be able to tell.

That is the honest state: permitted, unexercised, undetected.

What would fix it, in increasing order of effort:

  1. Set the customer realm’s user verification to required. Cheap, but it locks out authenticators that cannot verify — which is a real usability cost and may be why it was set to preferred.
  2. Inspect the amr claim or the UV flag in the access token and refuse AAL2-shaped operations when verification did not happen. Requires knowing what Keycloak actually emits.
  3. Accept it, and downgrade the claim: “sign-in is phishing-resistant” rather than “sign-in is multi-factor”. This is honest and may be enough for the product.

This analysis does not choose between them. That is a decision, not a finding.

Finding 2 — we do not use the Backup Eligible flag, and that is probably right

SP 800-63B Appendix B on Backup Eligible (BE):

“The Backup Eligible flag indicates whether the authenticator can be synced… Verifiers MAY use this flag to establish policies that restrict the use of syncable authenticators. This flag is necessary to distinguish authenticators that are device-bound from those that may be cloned.”

and on Backup State (BS):

“Agencies SHOULD NOT condition acceptance based on this flag for public-facing applications due to user experience concerns.”

We inspect neither. For the privileged realm that is fine, and arguably deliberate: an AAGUID allowlist plus cross-platform is stronger than a BE check, because it constrains which authenticator rather than merely whether it can sync. For the customer realm, BE would be the wrong tool — the standard permits syncing there.

So this is a non-finding, recorded so a reviewer can see it was considered rather than missed.

6. What remains for a human reviewer

The things this document cannot do for itself:

  1. Check the quotations against the source. They were copied from the HTML edition; a reviewer should confirm they match the PDF of record, and that the section numbering is right.
  2. Decide Finding 1. Set UV to required, inspect the claim, or downgrade the wording. The author should not make this call alone, because the author is the one who chose preferred.
  3. Confirm the AAL framing is one an auditor would accept. SP 800-63B is a US federal document; whether a SOC 2 or ISO 27001 assessor treats it as the reference is a question about them, not about the standard.
  4. Check the FIPS 140 position. AAL3 requires validated cryptographic modules. Whether the YubiKey 5 series we allowlist is validated in the configuration we permit is not established here, and it is a prerequisite for any AAL3 claim.

7. What changed as a result of this analysis

Nothing in the code. This slice produces a claim boundary, not a fix — with one exception to consider as a follow-up: the preferred UV setting in the customer realm is now a named, evidenced risk rather than an unexamined default, and it is recorded in EVIDENCE.md so it cannot be forgotten.
