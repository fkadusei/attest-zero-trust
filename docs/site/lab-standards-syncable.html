Reference: what the standard says about synced passkeys

The NIST analysis behind our claim boundary, quoted so a reviewer can check it.

Status: ANALYSIS COMPLETE AND MATERIALLY CORRECTED — awaiting independent HUMAN review.

S6 asked what the standards say about synced passkeys, and what we may claim publicly. This is the analysis.

An earlier draft of it overclaimed in several places, and a hostile review caught it. Section 7 records what the review found and what changed, because the correction is the most useful part of the document. The short version: the analysis audited itself against NIST and not against this project’s own list of failure patterns, and it repeated three of them.

Every quotation is traced to the primary source so a reviewer can check it without trusting the author. This is not an assessment. It is the input to one.

1. The primary source

NIST SP 800-63B-4, Digital Identity Guidelines: Authentication and Lifecycle Management, 2025-08-26.

  • §2.3 AAL3; §2.3.2 “Authenticator and Verifier Requirements”; §2.3.3 Reauthentication.
  • §3.1.6.1 Single-Factor Cryptographic Authenticators; §3.1.7.1 Multi-Factor Cryptographic Authenticators; §3.1.7.2 Multi-Factor Cryptographic Verifiers.
  • Appendix B, “Syncable Authenticators” — normative.

Sources: https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html and https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b/syncable/.

Scope. SP 800-63B is written for US federal agencies. It is the reference auditors reach for outside government too, but nothing here is a certification and this document does not claim compliance.

2. What the standard says about syncing

§2.3.2, on AAL3 — the clearest statement:

“Since syncable authenticators (described in Appendix B) require the private key to be exportable, syncable authenticators SHALL NOT be used at AAL3.”

The same section, positively:

“The cryptographic authenticator used at AAL3 SHALL have a non-exportable private key and SHALL provide phishing resistance…”

Appendix B.2, “Cloning Authentication Keys”:

“Syncing authentication keys inherently means that the key can be exported. Authentication at AAL2 may be supported subject to the above requirements. However, syncing violates the non-exportability requirements of AAL3. Similar protocols using keys not stored in an exportable manner that meet the other requirements of AAL3 may be used.”

§3.1.6.1 (single-factor) and §3.1.7.1 (multi-factor), quoted separately rather than stitched:

§3.1.6.1: “Exportable authentication keys (usable at AAL2 or below) SHOULD be stored in appropriate storage… Non-exportable authentication keys (usable at AAL3 or below) SHALL be stored in an isolated execution environment that is protected by hardware…”

§3.1.7.1: “Non-exportable authentication keys, suitable for use at AAL3, SHALL be stored in an isolated execution environment that is protected by hardware…”

So a synced passkey cannot be an AAL3 authenticator. Not because it is weak — because sync is export by definition. That part is unambiguous and does not depend on our configuration.

But subject to the above requirements is doing real work, and the earlier draft dropped it. Appendix B.2 imposes seven obligations on an AAL2 syncable deployment: approved cryptography; keys encrypted at 112-bit strength in the sync fabric; private-key operations performed on-device; access control so only the authenticated user reaches the keys; AAL2-equivalent MFA gating access to the sync fabric; the requirements documented and communicated, including on public-facing websites; and a UI showing what is synced where.

We satisfy none of these deliberately, because we are not the sync fabric. “The standard permits synced passkeys at AAL2” is therefore a statement about what the standard permits, not about what we have built. The distinction matters because that sentence is exactly what a DPO will read as an AAL2 claim.

3. What our configuration actually is

Read from lab/keycloak/scripts/configure-realms.py, which asserts each setting persisted. These are the webAuthnPolicyPasswordless* keys only — see F3.

Setting Customer realm (attest-users) Privileged realm (attest-privileged)
Attestation conveyance none direct
Authenticator attachment not specified cross-platform
User verification preferred required
Discoverable credential required required
AAGUID allowlist [] — any authenticator YubiKey 5 series
Synced passkey possible? Yes, by design Intended: no
RP ID localhost (hardcoded; production value only in a comment) same

The privileged realm asks for a cross-platform authenticator matching a hardware AAGUID, with attestation, and user verification required. As a request to an honest browser, that is what the design intends.

As an enforced control it is weaker than it looks — and that is what the review found. F1 and F2.

4. What we may claim — CORRECTED

An earlier draft said “privileged access requires a hardware-bound authenticator… This is tested (S3)”. That was wrong, and wrong in the direction that matters most. What the evidence supports:

We may say

  • “Enrolment into the privileged realm refuses an authenticator whose AAGUID is not on the allowlist, and refuses a built-in authenticator.” Tested (S3), with controls. Note what it is not: a statement about enrolment, not about access.
  • “Privileged sign-in is passkey-only.” Tested (S5d), by policy and by test.
  • “Synced passkeys are permitted for customer accounts, where the assurance target is AAL2” — but read §2 above: the standard permits this conditionally, and we are not the party satisfying those conditions.
  • “WebAuthn credentials are scoped to the relying-party origin, which is the mechanism behind phishing resistance.” Structural, and tested on loopback (S9).

We must NOT say

  • “Privileged access requires a hardware key”, or “you used your key just now”. EVIDENCE §5.8 records that the privileged step-up flow is password plus WebAuthn as a conditional second factor, and yields “verified freshness and, today, no strength”. No physical hardware key has ever completed a sign-in in this lab — EVIDENCE lists acceptance of a real key as Unverified.
  • “AAL3 compliant”, “AAL3-shaped”, or “NIST AAL3”. See F5 for the four separate reasons.
  • “A physical hardware key is accepted.” Only refusal has been tested.
  • “Sign-in is phishing-resistant” unqualified. S9 ran on loopback; real-TLS phishing is Unverified; the property depends on RP-ID width, and the RP ID is hardcoded to localhost. The enrolment link (bearer token) and enrolment window (password) are not origin-bound at all.
  • “Verified by search.” EVIDENCE reserves Verified for an experiment with a control. A grep is neither. A claim resting on one is at best Documented.

5. Findings

F1 — The AAGUID allowlist has no attestation trust anchor

Keycloak treats the AAGUID as meaningful only after requiring a non-none attestation statement, and it validates the chain against a truststore (WebAuthnRegisterFactory → KeycloakTrustAnchorRepository). With none configured it falls back to NullCertPathTrustworthinessVerifier.

This repository configures no truststore: grep across lab/keycloak/ finds AcceptableAaguids and zero hits for trust store, trust anchor, or truststore. Yubico’s FIDO attestation root is not in the JRE cacerts.

So the control is either unanchored (a fabricated x5c chain would satisfy it) or it rejects genuine YubiKeys — which matches the lockout failure mode EVIDENCE already names. We do not know which.

This is the same class as CVE-2026-6856, where packed self-attestation bypassed AcceptableAaguids (fixed in 26.6.2; this lab runs 26.8.0 and is not exposed) — that CVE is not a live risk here, but it is proof the failure mode is real.

State: unestablished, and it is the load-bearing part of the only enrolment control we have. This cannot be closed by configuration alone; it needs a pinned Yubico FIDO root and a demonstration that a forged chain fails.

F2 — cross-platform is not enforcement

Keycloak’s own source says authenticatorAttachment is client-reported and not cryptographically verified — it is not in the attestation object. The privileged policy’s cross-platform requirement is a request to an honest browser, not a control.

What this changes: the earlier draft’s “refused if either is not satisfied” was wrong. Only the AAGUID comparison has server-side enforcement, and F1 is about whether that is anchored.

F3 — We configured one of two policy surfaces

webAuthnPolicyPasswordless* governs passwordless sign-in. Keycloak also has webAuthnPolicy*, which governs a webauthn-type credential used as a second factor. This repository sets zero non-passwordless webAuthnPolicy* keys, so that surface keeps Keycloak’s defaults — including an empty acceptable-AAGUID list.

Two consequences. Any path accepting a webauthn-type credential bypasses the allowlist. And an allowlist is a registration-time filter: tightening it does not retroactively revoke credentials already stored.

Whether such a path is reachable today depends on which flows are enabled, which this analysis has not established.

F4 — NIST’s actual threat table is what this document should discuss

Appendix B.6 ships Table 5. The earlier draft examined only the two optional WebAuthn flags (BE/BS) and declared a non-finding — the wrong artefact. The real threats, and why they matter for this product specifically:

Threat (Appendix B.6) Why it matters here
Unauthorized key use, or loss of control — syncable authenticators may be shared between users An approval trail that says “Alice approved” can be produced by anyone Alice shares her key with. Undetectable by the relying party
Sync fabric compromise Our customers’ Apple/Google account security becomes part of our authentication chain, invisibly
Unauthorized access to the sync fabric and recovery — synced keys are reachable via cloud account recovery, “which represent a potential weakness” The sharpest problem. An attacker who recovers a customer’s iCloud or Google account produces a valid, phishing-resistant, user-verified assertion. Our log says “Alice signed in”. A passkey event is evidence that that account authenticated, not that the named human did. Nothing in this design addresses attribution
Revocation — RP-specific keys cannot be centrally revoked like PKI certificates We cannot revoke at the sync fabric; we can revoke our credential record and wait for token expiry — which S8 records as unmeasured

This is the section a compliance reviewer reads first. For a product whose value is the integrity of “who did what”, F4’s third row is more serious than everything else in this document.

F5 — “AAL3-shaped” is not shape-accurate

Even granting the allowlist, AAL3 also requires:

  • FIPS 140 L1+ for authenticators. An AAGUID encodes a model, not a validation status. The FIPS-series key has its own AAGUID, so unless the allowlisted value is the FIPS variant, the realm may be configured to reject the only AAL3-eligible keys.
  • FIPS 140 L1+ for verifiers. This document’s earlier draft discussed only authenticator FIPS. AAL3 constrains the verifier’s cryptography too — self-hosted Keycloak on a stock JVM.
  • Authentication intent. The User Present flag is client-asserted, exactly like UV.
  • 12h overall / 15m inactivity reauthentication. Never checked; Keycloak’s default idle is 30m.

“AAL3-flavoured authenticator selection” is the most this configuration supports. “AAL3-shaped” implies the shape is right and one corner is wrong. Four corners are wrong.

F6 — User verification: real mechanism, wrong layer, and one fix that is impossible

The customer realm sets user verification to preferred. Appendix B.3:

“Verifiers SHALL indicate that UV is preferred and SHALL inspect responses to confirm the value of the UV flag. This indicates whether the authenticator can be treated as a multi-factor cryptographic authenticator. If the user is not verified, agencies SHALL treat the authenticator as a single-factor cryptographic authenticator.“

and §3.1.7.2:

“If such a flag is present and indicates that an activation factor was not used, the authentication SHALL be treated as single-factor.”

The mechanism is real, and it is stronger than the earlier draft knew. In Keycloak’s WebAuthnAuthenticator, isUVFlagChecked is set only when the policy is required. Under preferred the server accepts a UV=0 assertion and does not act on it. A customer may therefore authenticate with one factor while the system treats the sign-in as multi-factor.

But three things in the earlier draft were wrong:

  1. Wrong layer. The UV flag exists in the WebAuthn assertion, which only Keycloak sees. It is not in the access token, so “inspect the UV flag in the access token” was impossible to implement, and checking apps/api/src was checking the wrong place.
  2. Wrong remedy framing. NIST requires preferred — “Verifiers SHALL indicate that UV is preferred”. The deviation is the missing inspection, not the setting. Setting required would be a departure from prescribed behaviour, not an implementation of it. It is also the only server-side enforcement Keycloak offers, so calling it “cheap” understated it.
  3. Wrong strength of claim. Nothing has shown a real authenticator returning UV=0 in a residentKey: required flow. Honest browsers usually do UV under preferred; the live threat is a hostile client that suppresses it. The earlier draft’s “evidenced risk” was Assumed.

State: a real gap at the Keycloak layer, conditional, unmeasured.

F7 — The configuration script can brick the admin realm mid-run

configure-realms.py writes ["not-a-guid"] into the live privileged realm’s allowlist to prove the setting is writable, then restores it. An interruption between those two points leaves a realm that accepts no authenticator at all. Undisclosed until now — and worth fixing regardless of what the standards say.

6. What the review did not overturn

Recorded so a reviewer knows what survived: the AAL3/exportability reading in §2 is correct and well-cited; refusing to claim AAL3 or a blanket AAL2 is right; and the two-realm architecture itself is sound. The objection is to the evidence for calling it AAL3-shaped, not to the split.

7. What the review changed, and the pattern behind it

Two independent adversarial passes ran. The first checked every quotation against the source; the second attacked the reasoning and then checked Keycloak’s source, which this document should have done itself instead of grepping apps/api/src.

Quotation defects fixed: §2.3.3 cited where §2.3.2 was meant; a “§3.1.7.1” quote that was actually §3.1.6.1, reordered across an ellipsis; a Backup Eligible quote truncated mid-sentence.

Substantive defects fixed: the §4 hardware-key claim (→ §4, F1–F2); the missing attestation trust anchor (F1); the second policy surface (F3); the absent threat table (F4); “AAL3-shaped” (F5); the mislocated and over-strong user-verification finding (F6); the undisclosed script behaviour (F7).

The pattern is the important part. Each substantive defect is one this project has already named in EVIDENCE §2a, and the document repeated three of them:

EVIDENCE §2a pattern How this document repeated it
A check that cannot fail “Verified by search” — a grep presented at experiment strength
The wrong subject Enrolment tested, access claimed; the passwordless policy described, the realm claimed
Self-vouching The configuration script’s success message treated as evidence of a security property

The document was audited against NIST. It was not audited against that list. That is the finding worth keeping, and it applies to the next document too.

8. What remains for a human reviewer

  1. Establish the trust-anchor position (F1). Until it is resolved, “hardware key required” cannot be said. Needs a pinned Yubico root and a forged-chain test.
  2. Decide F6. Accept and downgrade the wording to “phishing-resistant”; investigate what can be enforced at the verifier; or treat customer sign-in as single-factor and say so. The author should not choose, because the author set preferred.
  3. Determine whether the webauthn credential surface (F3) is reachable, then configure or disable it.
  4. Answer F4’s attribution question. An evidence system whose log says “Alice did this” when the actor may be whoever holds Alice’s sync fabric is a product problem before it is a standards problem. This document does not solve it.
  5. Confirm the quotations against the PDF of record, and whether an auditor accepts the AAL framing at all.

9. What changed in the code as a result of S6

Nothing. S6 produces a claim boundary. The one code-adjacent consequence is F7, which is a bug worth fixing regardless.
